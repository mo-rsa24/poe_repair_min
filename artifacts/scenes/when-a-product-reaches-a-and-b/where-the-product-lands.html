Where these numbers come from

The toy. One Gaussian blob per quadrant at (±0.5, ±0.5), standard deviation 0.15. A is x₁ > 0 (pneumothorax), B is x₂ > 0 (no chest drain). Top-right is A and B, pneumothorax without a drain. Bottom-right is A, not B (pneumothorax with a drain). Top-left is B, not A (no pneumothorax, no drain). Bottom-left is neither (a drain without pneumothorax).

How the numbers were made. The experts are ideal blob mixtures with chosen weights, multiplied on a 441 × 441 grid over [−1.1, 1.1]². There is no training and no sampler. The walk's answer key is artifacts/ideas/when-poe-reaches-a-and-b/runs/answer-key-quadrants/answer_key.py, run in the co3 environment on 2026-10-17. This page's key, artifacts/scenes/when-a-product-reaches-a-and-b/checks/scene_key.py, copies that script's grid and functions unchanged and reproduces all 18 of its rows. It adds only the slider stops the walk's key does not print (ε = 0.2, 0.15, 0.075, 0.025, 0.005; leftover next door at every stop; r = 0.1, 0.02, 0.005) and the four drag positions of press 3.

What "in the top-right blob" counts. The share of the normalised composed density within 3 standard deviations (0.45) of (0.5, 0.5), the dashed ring. "At the origin" is within 0.45/√2 ≈ 0.318 of (0, 0). A quadrant share would mislead: two far-apart blobs multiply into one blob between them, which a quadrant count would split.

Why 0.989 keeps appearing. One data blob keeps 1 − e^(−9/2) = 0.9889 of its mass inside its own 3-std ring. The background-divided product in 2b rebuilds the top-right data blob exactly, so it reads 0.989. Construction (1) comes extremely close to that blob for every r > 0 listed, because the other blobs meet it only through tiny tails: 0.9889 at r = 0.25 and 0.9886 at r = 0.001. It is not exactly the same density.

Why 2b's plain product reads 0.9999. Write φ(u) for the data blob's profile N(u; 0.5, 0.15²) and b(u) for the background marginal. Expert A is φ(x₁)·b(x₂) and expert B is b(x₁)·φ(x₂), so their product is [φ(x₁)b(x₁)]·[φ(x₂)b(x₂)]. Near top-right b(u) is half of φ(u), so each coordinate goes as φ(u)², a Gaussian with half the variance. That narrower blob puts more than 0.989 inside the ring. Dividing by b(x₁)b(x₂) cancels both background factors exactly and leaves φ(x₁)φ(x₂), the data blob. So 0.9999 is not a better fit than 0.989.

Support on this toy. A Gaussian blob is never exactly zero, so "outside the support" here means smaller by many orders of magnitude: the product's raw mass Z falls from 3.54 (both blobs on bottom-right) to 7.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ (opposite corners). Normalised, that leftover sits where the two tails meet: the product of two Gaussians is a Gaussian between them (no textbook page linked: this machine has no PDF reader to confirm one). The vault note "Diffusion Models", section "When neither training set holds A and B", runs the same rule with an exactly-zero bar: there Z falls from 0.313 to 1.6 × 10⁻⁷. Each panel's shading is linear in density and scaled to that panel's own peak; press 3's "raw" view scales the product to the case where both blobs overlap.

What ε is and is not. ε is the weight each fine-tuned expert keeps on each quadrant outside its own. It is not a count of fine-tuning steps: how fast fine-tuning lowers ε is check 2a in the walk's idea map, and it has not run. At ε = 0.25 both experts equal the pretrained model (fine-tune step 0); at ε = 0 they are experts trained from scratch on one quadrant each. The formula ε/(2 − 4ε) comes from treating each blob as a weight per quadrant and ignoring products between blobs at different centres: top-right and bottom-left get ε², bottom-right and top-left get ε(1 − 3ε), the total is 2ε − 4ε², and ε² / (2ε − 4ε²) = ε/(2 − 4ε) for ε > 0; the grid matches it to four decimal places down to ε = 0.001. At ε = 0 the formula says 0 and the grid says 0.006, because the blobs' tails meeting at the origin take over.

Construction (1) is one conditional model with "contains A" and "contains B" labels, composed as p(x|A)·p(x|B)/p(x), with a share r of all films in top-right and (1 − r)/3 on each other quadrant. The key multiplies the joint densities p(x, A) and p(x, B) rather than the conditionals; the constants between them cancel on rescaling, so every share is the same, but the raw mass Z is the joint version's. That Z is close to r for r > 0 (0.250, 0.0500, 0.0100, 0.00100 in the key) and 3.8 × 10⁻⁷ at r = 0; true conditionals would divide it by (r + (1 − r)/3)². Rescue 2b builds the factorisation in: expert A models x₁ from bottom-right films only and borrows x₂ from a background marginal; expert B is the mirror image. The key's background marginals put half their mass at each of ±0.5. Those marginals need no film that is jointly A and B, since films with x₂ near 0.5 exist in top-left. With top-right films absent the split would move off one half; by hand, neither 2b number depends on it, because division cancels the background exactly and in the plain product the background only rescales the top-right blob (not run). Trained 2D networks did not learn this factorisation on their own in the earlier walk (they matched their smoothed data within 0.04 nats). The background-divided form is projective composition, Bradley et al. 2025a, arXiv 2502.04549. Rescue 2c needs the sampler runs of claim 4, which have not run, so the sampler slot is empty on purpose.

Two kinds of p_A. In construction (2), pA is the expert fine-tuned on bottom-right films only, which learned "not B" too. In construction (1), the factors are p(x | A) and p(x | B), which include the top-right films. The two are not interchangeable, which is the whole difference between 0.0278 and 0.989 at the same rarity.

Samplers. Against an exact target there is no model error, but a sampler's histogram can still miss through finite samples, discretisation or a bug, so claim 4 reads the gap against the perfect-sampler floor.
