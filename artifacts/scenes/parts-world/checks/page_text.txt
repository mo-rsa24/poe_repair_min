## Disclosure: where these numbers come from
Where these numbers come from
The toy. Every image is 16 numbers drawn as a 4 × 4 tile: pixels 0 to 7 are the top two rows, the head, and 8 to 15 the bottom two rows, the body. The left half is the left two columns. The cat expert's mean is +1 and the dog's −1. Sure means variance 0.01 and loose means variance 1. Every expert is a Gaussian with a diagonal covariance, or in the position chapter a mixture of two, so every product on this page is exact, with no grid and no sampler.
The one identity. For two diagonal Gaussians, N(x; μa, Σa) N(x; μb, Σb) = Z N(x; μ, Σ), with Σ = (Σa−1 + Σb−1)−1, μ = Σ(Σa−1μa + Σb−1μb) and Z = N(μa; μb, Σa + Σb): precisions add and the mean is precision-weighted, pixel by pixel. Textbook: the Matrix Cookbook, section 8.1.8, page 42. log Z is the sum of the 16 pixels' logs, because the log of a product is the sum of the logs (algebra handbook, page 110). Per pixel, log N(μa; μb, va + vb) = −½[log(2π(va + vb)) + (μa − μb)²/(va + vb)]. A sure +1 against a sure −1: −½[log(2π × 0.02) + 4/0.02] = −98.962927. A sure +1 against a loose −1: −½[log(2π × 1.01) + 4/1.01] = −2.904112. A sure ±1 against a loose 0 adds −1.418963; a loose 0 against a loose 0 adds −1.265512. So with the dog sure on the head too, log Z = 8 × (−98.962927) + 8 × (−2.904112) = −814.936. With means +1 and −1 the product's mean on a pixel simplifies to (vdog − vcat)/(vdog + vcat): (1 − 0.01)/(1 + 0.01) = 99/101 on the head, and the sign flips on the body. Z is the area under the product, not a share, so it is not bounded by 1: two narrow experts that agree give log Z above 0. "Higher is more overlap" holds on this page because the means stay at +1 and −1.
Mixtures. A product of two Gaussian mixtures has one component per pair of components, with log weight before normalising ℓij = log πi + log ρj + log N(μi; νj, Σi + Λj) and weight wij = eℓij / Σkl eℓkl (mixtures: Mathematics for Machine Learning, page 355). Each placement has weight 0.5, so every pair starts at log 0.25 = −1.386294. A side-by-side pair adds 16 × (−1.418963) = −22.703, giving ℓ = −24.090. A same-half pair adds 8 × (−98.962927) + 8 × (−1.265512) = −801.828, giving ℓ = −803.214. The gap is Δ = 779.124; write r = e−Δ. The four weights sum to 2eℓside(1 + r), so each side-by-side pair has w = 1/[2(1 + r)] and each same-half pair w = r/[2(1 + r)]. Both are positive; at two decimals they print as 0.50 and 0.00, which the page writes as ≈ 0.50 and ≈ 0.00. A side-by-side component's mean is 100/101 = +0.990 on the cat's half and −0.990 on the dog's.
Noise. The forward process turns N(μ, v) into N(√ᾱ μ, ᾱ v + 1 − ᾱ). At ᾱ = 0.5 a cat head pixel becomes N(0.707, 0.505) and a dog head pixel N(−0.707, 1.0), so the cat's precision lead falls from 100 : 1 to 1.980 : 1. At that one level, cat score + dog score is the score of the density proportional to the noised cat times the noised dog, whose head mean is (1.980 × 0.707 − 1 × 0.707) / 2.980 = 0.233. The correct intermediate target is the noised product, √0.5 × 0.980 = 0.693. This is the error Du et al. (2023), section 4, equations 11 and 12, describe, computed exactly here. This fixed-level mean does not by itself say what a full reverse run produces, and how much of the mosaic such a run keeps at ᾱ = 1 is not computed. The two families differ even at full noise: each expert becomes N(0, 1), their product is proportional to N(0, ½), and the noised clean product is N(0, 1). Noise is drawn only with position fixed; the mixture case under noise is not on this page.
The three draws under each tile are illustrative: each is the tile's mean plus its standard deviation times one fixed standard-normal number per pixel (seed 9), so they move smoothly as you drag. Colours clip at ±1. They show which pixels an expert is sure of, not any measured sample.
