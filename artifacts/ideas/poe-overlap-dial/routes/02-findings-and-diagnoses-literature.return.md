## 2. Per term: supports, splits or contradicts

**Finding: supported, with the boundary drawn in a different place.** PadChest defines a finding as anything "radiologists can assert based solely on the interpretation of a chest x-ray image" (p.10), and that rule admits diagnoses that are visible on the film, such as pneumothorax. The project draws its line between a sign and a disease, while PadChest draws it between what the image alone settles and what needs the patient's history. The Fleischner glossary keeps a third label, the descriptor, for terms that say what is seen without implying a cause: consolidation "is a descriptor that does not indicate the pathologic nature of the condition" (entry: Consolidation). The datasets disagree on single labels. Pulmonary oedema is a finding in the project and an "observation" in CheXpert, and it sits in PadChest's differential diagnosis tree (p.29). Cardiomegaly is a finding in PadChest as in the project (p.27). ChestX-ray8 and CheXpert do not keep the split at all.

**Diagnosis built from findings: split, and the "close to a fixed function" claim is contradicted.** The structure holds: heart failure is read from cardiomegaly, effusion and oedema, and Pan et al. list those signs as the ones that "start to appear" as pressure rises (Introduction). The fixed-function claim does not hold. PadChest says diagnoses need "clinical information and/or additional studies" beyond the image (p.10). Pan et al. say "The CXR is not a diagnostic test for HF" (Introduction), and that not all patients with acute heart failure show congestion. Among their 975 patients each sign was present in 64% to 78% of films (Abstract). Kobayashi et al. adjudicated heart failure from charts, echocardiography and natriuretic peptides, and the radiograph score separated it with AUC 0.84 (Results). So the image determines the findings, and the findings constrain the diagnosis without fixing it. The causal arrow runs from diagnosis to findings, and the reading arrow runs back with uncertainty. A second kind of "built from" also appears: Pham et al.'s hierarchy is an is-a relation ("Cardiomegaly implies the presence of Enlarged Cardiomediastinum", p.8), and PadChest's trees are the same ("chronic tuberculosis" is "tuberculosis", p.10). That relation is subsumption: a child label implies its parent.

**Comorbidity: split.** No source reserves "comorbidity" or "co-occurrence" for two diagnoses that leave each other's appearance alone. Kobayashi et al. use "comorbidities" for obesity and COPD beside heart failure (Results). Lin et al. use it for any correlated pair of labels (abstract only). Ehrlich et al. call silicotuberculosis "co-occurrence of silicosis and TB" (Plain chest radiography), which is the project's compound case. ChestX-ray8 calls label co-occurrence "the interactions and relationships among objects" (p.3), so the field's "interaction" can mean the project's co-occurrence. The "no effect on appearance" part of the project's definition is the project's own addition. Kobayashi et al. give one hint that some comorbidities do leave the reading alone: the congestion score performed about the same with or without COPD and pneumonia (section "Radiographic congestion score index as a diagnostic tool for acute heart failure").

**Compound disease: supported, with the visible effect named in three parts.** Ehrlich et al. define silicotuberculosis as "the combination of silicosis and active TB and/or post-tuberculous lung disease" (Definitions). They name a shared mechanism: silica damages the macrophage, "the frontline of innate defence against silica and M. tuberculosis" (Co-pathogenesis). That matches the project's "two causes acting on the same tissue". On the radiograph, the review describes three effects. One disease masks the other: "early nodular changes of TB may be difficult to identify, especially if silicosis is evident", and "silicosis may be missed in the presence of tuberculous abnormalities". One changes the other's pattern: TB modifies the "angel wing" distribution, and silica exposure may give "a more profuse tuberculous nodulation" (both Plain chest radiography). And combined disease can make a lesion neither gives alone: TB "contributes to conglomeration of discrete silicotic nodules" into masses (Pathology). Their summary is that silicotuberculosis is "a phenotype in which clear distinction between the presentation and effects of the two diseases may not always be possible" (Aetiology). The project's "a pattern neither gives alone" is the third effect. The first two (masking and distortion) are also compound effects, and they are the ones a reader meets most.

**Consequence: supported for the data, contradicted for appearance.** Oakden-Rayner et al. call the drain "a non-causal image feature in the setting of pneumothorax, as this device is the common form of treatment" and "a healthcare process variable" (p.3 to 4). That matches the project's "consequence" and its link to shortcuts. The project also says the dependence is "in the data, not in appearance", and the same author contradicts that. In NIH, "the majority of pneumothorax cases were already treated with chest tubes and often did not show radiographic evidence of ectopic pleural gas" (Oakden-Rayner 2019, p.7). Treatment re-expands the lung, so the consequence changes the image of the diagnosis and can erase the pleural line the label promises. In the project's own vocabulary that is the temporal kind: the cause has passed and its trace (the drain) remains. Rueckel et al. add that drains are a consequence of more than pneumothorax: 293 of 2,302 pneumothorax-negative supine films (12.7%) also had one (Table 1). PadChest files every tube and drain in its radiographic findings tree (p.28), so in dataset terms a consequence is also a finding.

## 3. The chest drain rate among NIH pneumothorax images

The rate depends on which images are counted and who labelled them. Three measurements exist.

| Rate | Images | Who labelled the drain | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 80% | pneumothorax-positive images in the test set of a model that reproduces CheXNet; the count is not stated | one board-certified radiologist | Oakden-Rayner et al. 2019, *Hidden Stratification*, p.3: "We find that 80% of pneumothoraces in the test set contained a chest drain" (quoted) |
| 80% | pneumothorax-positive images from a visual review of ChestX-ray14 (exploratory review of about 100 per class, relabelling of 50 per class) | the same radiologist | Oakden-Rayner 2019, *Exploring large scale public medical image datasets*, p.5: "80% of the positive cases have chest drains" (quoted) |
| 46.2% (1,637 of 3,543) | NIH pneumothorax-labelled images outside the radiologist's set and outside those patients' images; 1,011 patients | two non-expert annotators, kept only where both were certain | counted here from the NEATX release of Cheplygina et al. 2025 (Zenodo record 14944064), joined to the NIH label file |

**The 46.2% count broken down** (counted here). All 3,543 annotated images carry the NIH Pneumothorax label. That is 3,543 of NIH's 5,302 pneumothorax images. By view, the rate is 44.6% on PA films (1,030 of 2,311) and 49.3% on AP films (607 of 1,232). Taking only each patient's earliest annotated image gives 34.1% (345 of 1,011). Cheplygina et al. validated these labels by training a drain detector on them and testing it on the radiologist's held-out labels (p.7). They report κ = 0.72 against PadChest's own drain labels (p.7), and print no κ against the radiologist on NIH.

**Why 80% and 46% may both be right.** They cover different images: Cheplygina et al. removed the radiologist's images and every other image of the same patients before annotating (p.4), so the two sets share no patients. The NEATX set is also dominated by follow-up films: 3,543 images from 1,011 patients. A second explanation is that non-experts miss drains, which would make 46.2% a lower bound. Nothing found here separates the two explanations (inferred).

**Outside NIH.** Rueckel et al. counted drains on 677 unilateral pneumothoraces in their own supine films: 507 (74.9%) had one (Table 1, quoted). For bilateral pneumothoraces the figure is 74 of 83 (89.2%).

**For the paper.** The honest sentence is "80% of pneumothorax-positive NIH test images carry a chest drain (Oakden-Rayner et al. 2019, radiologist review), and about half of the remaining pneumothorax images do (46.2% of 3,543, counted here from Cheplygina et al.'s non-expert labels)".

## 4. Does any chest X-ray generator compose findings from separately trained models?

No, as far as these searches reach. Every generator found is one model given several findings at once in a prompt or report.

**RoentGen** is one Stable Diffusion fine-tuned on MIMIC-CXR. It is "capable of inserting, combining and modifying imaging appearances of various CXR findings through free-form medical language text prompts" (p.2, quoted). Its combined examples are joint prompts such as "Big right-sided pleural effusion with adjacent atelectasis" (Figure 5, p.13). Its retrieval test keeps only reports with a single abnormality ("we discard reports with multiple labels", p.12).

**Cheff** is one cascade "conditioned on radiological reports" (abstract, p.1, quoted).

**RoentMod** edits one finding at a time with RoentGen's weights. It reports two composition failures. Generated scans leak co-occurrence: "cardiomegaly often appeared regardless of the user prompt" (p.7). A classifier trained on its one-finding edits shows "negative shortcut learning, underpredicting findings when another is present" (p.5). The authors suggest "Generating multiple pathologies on a single image" as the fix (p.7, all quoted).

**CompDiff** is the only chest X-ray paper that calls itself compositional. It composes separately encoded demographic tokens (age, sex, race) inside one jointly trained network, and it generates 16 age, sex and race combinations left out of training (p.1 to 4). It lists Composable Diffusion ("Composition of conditional scores at sampling") in its comparison table under "Object and scene combinations" (p.4) and does not apply it to chest X-rays.

**Searched without a hit.** arXiv: "product of experts" with chest X-ray; "composable diffusion" with medical; "compositional generation" with chest X-ray; "score composition" with radiograph; "compositional generalization" with chest; diffusion with LoRA and pathology. Europe PMC: "product of experts", "composable diffusion" or "compositional generation" with chest radiograph or chest x-ray. The finding is "no paper found by these queries", which is weaker than "no such paper exists".

## 5. From the literature: paragraphs ready to paste

Each citation carries a flag in brackets: **quoted** (read in full text at the location given), **counted here**, **inferred**, or **abstract only**.

**Finding.** Radiology separates what the film shows from what causes it, and the datasets draw that line in different places. PadChest defines a radiographic finding as anything "radiologists can assert based solely on the interpretation of a chest x-ray image" (Bustos et al. 2020, p.10) [quoted]. This puts pneumothorax among the findings, because the image alone settles it, and cardiomegaly and pleural effusion there too (p.27) [quoted]. The Fleischner glossary labels terms as anatomy, physiology or "an abnormal finding", and marks some as descriptors "to differentiate them from terms that imply a radiologic diagnosis"; consolidation, for example, "does not indicate the pathologic nature of the condition" (Bankier et al. 2024, Introduction and Consolidation) [quoted]. CheXpert calls its labels "observations" and says it kept pneumonia "despite being a clinical diagnosis" (Irvin et al. 2019, p.2) [quoted]. ChestX-ray8 calls every label a "thoracic disease" and merges the UMLS types "Diseases or Syndromes" and "Findings" (Wang et al. 2017, p.3) [quoted]. Pulmonary oedema shows the disagreement: it is an observation in CheXpert and a differential diagnosis in PadChest (p.29) [quoted].

**Diagnosis built from findings.** A diagnosis is read from findings, and the findings do not fix it. PadChest keeps diagnoses in their own tree because they need "the patient's clinical information and/or additional studies" beyond the image, and it files heart insufficiency there (Bustos et al. 2020, p.10 and p.29) [quoted]. For heart failure the signs on the film are the ones the project names: as pulmonary pressure rises, "pulmonary venous congestion, interstitial oedema, alveolar oedema and pleural effusions" appear (Pan et al. 2021, Introduction) [quoted]. The same paper states that "The CXR is not a diagnostic test for HF" and that not every acute heart failure patient shows congestion. Among 975 admitted patients, venous congestion appeared on 78% of films, Kerley B lines on 71%, effusions on 67% and alveolar oedema on 64% (Abstract) [quoted]. When heart failure is adjudicated from the whole record, a radiographic congestion score separates it with an AUC of 0.84 (Kobayashi et al. 2020, Results) [quoted]. The findings are evidence for the diagnosis and the diagnosis causes the findings, so the composition runs one way in generation and the other way, with uncertainty, in reading [inferred]. The label hierarchies in the field are a different relation, subsumption: "Cardiomegaly implies the presence of Enlarged Cardiomediastinum" (Pham et al., p.8) [quoted].

**Comorbidity.** The field uses "comorbidity" and "co-occurrence" for any two conditions present together and says nothing about whether one alters the other's appearance. Kobayashi et al. (2020, Results) call obesity and COPD comorbidities of heart failure [quoted]. Lin et al. (2026) use the word for correlated labels in chest X-ray classifiers [abstract only]. Ehrlich et al. (2024, Plain chest radiography) speak of the "co-occurrence of silicosis with TB" for a pair that does alter appearance [quoted]. ChestX-ray8 describes label co-occurrence as "the interactions and relationships among objects" (Wang et al. 2017, p.3) [quoted]. The project's narrower meaning (two diagnoses with no effect on each other's look) therefore needs stating as its own definition. One piece of evidence fits it: a radiographic heart failure score performed about the same with or without COPD and pneumonia (Kobayashi et al. 2020, section "Radiographic congestion score index as a diagnostic tool for acute heart failure") [quoted].

**Compound disease.** Silicotuberculosis is "the combination of silicosis and active TB and/or post-tuberculous lung disease" (Ehrlich et al. 2024, Definitions) [quoted]. The two share a mechanism, because silica damages the macrophage, "the frontline of innate defence against silica and M. tuberculosis" (Co-pathogenesis) [quoted]. The review describes three effects on the radiograph. Each disease can hide the other: "early nodular changes of TB may be difficult to identify, especially if silicosis is evident", and "silicosis may be missed in the presence of tuberculous abnormalities" [quoted]. Each can change the other's pattern, as when TB modifies the symmetrical "angel wing" spread of silicotic nodules and silica exposure gives "a more profuse tuberculous nodulation" (Plain chest radiography) [quoted]. Together they can build a lesion neither builds alone, as when the tuberculous process "contributes to conglomeration of discrete silicotic nodules" into masses (Pathology) [quoted]. The review's summary is that combined disease is "a phenotype in which clear distinction between the presentation and effects of the two diseases may not always be possible" (Aetiology) [quoted].

**Consequence.** A drain follows a pneumothorax because it treats it. Oakden-Rayner et al. call it "a non-causal image feature in the setting of pneumothorax" and "a healthcare process variable" (2019, p.3 to 4) [quoted]. In NIH ChestX-ray14, 80% of pneumothorax-positive test images carry a drain (p.3) [quoted]. Among 3,543 other pneumothorax-labelled NIH images, 1,637 (46.2%) do, by non-expert labels (Cheplygina et al. 2025, labels from their Zenodo release) [counted here]. A classifier's pneumothorax AUC is 0.94 on images with drains and 0.77 on images without (Oakden-Rayner et al. 2019, p.3) [quoted]. The consequence also changes the image of the cause. Most NIH pneumothorax cases "were already treated with chest tubes and often did not show radiographic evidence of ectopic pleural gas" (Oakden-Rayner 2019, p.7) [quoted], so a drain image can carry the label with no pleural line to see. Drains are a consequence of more than one diagnosis: 12.7% of pneumothorax-negative supine films in one hospital cohort had a thoracic tube (Rueckel et al. 2021, Table 1) [quoted]. Generators learn these dependencies as well. In RoentMod's edits, "cardiomegaly often appeared regardless of the user prompt" (Cooke et al. 2025, p.7) [quoted].

## What the reading suggests for the [proposed] medical entries

These go to the "Proposed changes from the literature" section of the composition terms when the write-back runs. None is applied.

1. **Finding.** Replace "a visible sign, not a disease" with PadChest's test: what the image alone settles. Keep "descriptor" (Fleischner) for a sign that names no cause. Note that pulmonary oedema is a finding in one scheme and a diagnosis in another.
2. **Diagnosis built from findings.** Drop "close to a fixed function of the findings". Say the diagnosis causes the findings and is read back from them with uncertainty, plus information that is not in the image. This affects claim 7, which uses heart failure as the case where the interaction term is zero. The literature supports that only for the findings given the diagnosis. It does not support it for the diagnosis given the findings.
3. **Comorbidity.** Keep the project's definition but mark it as narrower than the field's word.
4. **Compound disease.** Name the three effects: masking, distortion of the other's pattern, and a new lesion.
5. **Consequence.** Remove "not in appearance". A treated pneumothorax looks different from an untreated one, so the consequence is temporal as well as associative. Also add that one device can be the consequence of several diagnoses.
6. **"Which kind a pair is".** The drain and pneumothorax pair is co-occurrence in pixels, associative in the data (NIH: 46% to 80% depending on the subset) and temporal in appearance (treated lungs re-expand).
