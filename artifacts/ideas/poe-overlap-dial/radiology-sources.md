# Radiology sources behind the composition terms

These are quoted radiology facts that ground the chest X-ray composition terms used in this idea folder. Each fact below gives the exact quote, the page it came from, that site's licence, and whether the fact was found. Pages were read on 15 October 2026.

**How the quotes were checked**

Radiology Assistant pages were downloaded as raw HTML and searched as text, so those quotes are character-exact. Radiopaedia blocks scripted downloads with a bot check, so its quotes came through WebFetch, which returns at most 125 characters per quote; a longer sentence arrives as consecutive pieces, which are joined here and marked "joined from pieces". These Radiopaedia quotes are worth a glance on the live page before going into a paper.

**1. The silhouette sign: lost borders, heart borders, effusions**

Status: verified for the definition and heart borders. Not found for effusions: neither site's silhouette text mentions pleural effusion, and the Radiopaedia pleural effusion article does not contain the word "silhouette".

Radiopaedia, "Silhouette sign (x-ray)", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/silhouette-sign-x-ray (last revised 2 Sep 2026), CC BY-NC-SA 3.0:

> "The silhouette sign denotes that juxtaposed structures or pathology of similar radiographic attenuation can obscure an anatomical border or interface" (joined from pieces; citation marker omitted)

> "This can be pathological (e.g. consolidation obscuring a heart border) or non-pathological (e.g. pectus excavatum obscuring the right heart border, hiatus hernia obscuring the posterior heart border)" (joined from pieces)

List items on the same page map each lost border to a lobe: "right heart border: right middle lobe or medial right lower lobe", "left heart border: lingular segments of the left upper lobe", "right hemidiaphragm: right lower lobe", "left hemidiaphragm or descending aorta: left lower lobe".

Radiology Assistant, "Chest X-Ray: Basic Interpretation", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/chest-x-ray/basic-interpretation, no licence stated:

> "The silhouette sign refers to the loss of the normal border between structures of different radiographic densities, most commonly air-filled lung adjacent to soft tissue structures like the heart or diaphragm."

> "When consolidation is present in the lingula, which directly abuts the left heart border, the silhouette of the left ventricle becomes obscured."

Radiology Assistant, "Chest X-Ray: Lung disease", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/chest-x-ray/lung-disease, no licence stated:

> "Silhouette sign: loss of lung/soft tissue interface"

> "Blurring of the right heart border (silhouette sign)"

The two sites word the definition from opposite ends. Radiopaedia says the two things touching have similar attenuation; Radiology Assistant says the border lost is between structures of different densities (air and soft tissue), which is lost when the air is replaced.

**2. Subpulmonic effusion mimics an elevated hemidiaphragm**

Status: verified on both sites.

Radiopaedia, "Subpulmonic effusion", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/subpulmonic-effusion (last revised 8 Sep 2026), CC BY-NC-SA 3.0:

> "Subpulmonic effusions, also known as subpulmonary effusions, are seen on erect chest radiographs when pleural effusions form a layer between the lung base and the diaphragm." (joined from pieces)

> "The subpulmonic fluid mimics an elevated diaphragm."

> "When this occurs on the left, the distance between the lung and the gastric bubble increases" (reference marker omitted)

Radiopaedia, "Elevated hemidiaphragm", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/elevated-hemidiaphragm, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0, under its Differential diagnosis heading:

> "Other situations which may mimic an elevated hemidiaphragm include:" followed by the list item "subpulmonic effusion"

Radiology Assistant, "Chest X-Ray: Heart Failure", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/chest-x-ray/heart-failure, no licence stated:

> "A subpulmonic effusion may follow the contour of the diaphragm making it tricky to discern."

> "At first glance you might get the impression that there is a high position of the diaphragm."

> "However when you notice the increased distance of the stomach air bubble to the lung base, you realize that there is a large amount of pleural fluid on both sides (arrow)."

**3. Does cardiomegaly elevate or depress a hemidiaphragm, or compress the left lower lobe?**

Status: not found. Neither site says cardiomegaly elevates or depresses a hemidiaphragm, and neither says it compresses or collapses the left lower lobe. What the sources do say is below, and it runs in the other direction or concerns detection.

Radiopaedia, "Elevated hemidiaphragm", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/elevated-hemidiaphragm, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0. The cause list has three headings: "above the diaphragm" (decreased lung volume, atelectasis/collapse, prior lobectomy or pneumonectomy, pulmonary hypoplasia, pulmonary fibrosis), "diaphragm" (phrenic nerve palsy, neuromuscular disease, eventration, contralateral stroke and others) and "below the diaphragm" (abdominal tumour, subphrenic abscess, organomegaly, distended viscus, Chilaiditi sign). The words "cardiomegaly" and "heart" do not appear in the article body.

Radiopaedia, "Compressive atelectasis", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/compressive-atelectasis, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0:

> "Compressive atelectasis refers to a form of lung atelectasis due to compression by a space-occupying process."

Its causes are pleural (effusion, empyema, haemothorax, pneumothorax, pleural tumour, chest wall mass), intrapulmonary (lung cancer, bullous disease) and abdominal distension. Cardiomegaly is not in the article body.

Radiopaedia, "Left lower lobe collapse", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/left-lower-lobe-collapse, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0. Cardiomegaly appears only as something that makes the collapse harder to see:

> "Left lower lobe collapse is readily identified on a well-penetrated radiograph of a patient with a normal sized heart but can be challenging in the typical patient with collapse, namely unwell patients, with portable (AP) often underpenetrated films, or with concomitant cardiomegaly." (joined from pieces)

The same article lists "elevation of the left hemidiaphragm" among non-specific signs of the collapse itself, so a raised left hemidiaphragm next to a big heart has a route through left lower lobe collapse, with the collapse as the cause.

Radiology Assistant, "Chest X-Ray: Heart Failure", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/chest-x-ray/heart-failure. The only heart-and-diaphragm sentence found runs from diaphragm to heart:

> "On a supine film the cardiac silhouette will be larger due to magnification and high position of the hemidiafragms."

Radiology Assistant, "Chest X-Ray: Basic Interpretation", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/chest-x-ray/basic-interpretation. A cardiac-adjacent structure can imitate a raised hemidiaphragm:

> "On chest radiography, a pericardial cyst may mimic an elevated left hemidiaphragm (black arrow)."

Leads not quoted: a Radiopaedia case found by search (https://radiopaedia.org/cases/84206) is described in search snippets as cardiomegaly with left diaphragmatic elevation in an obese patient, and another case (https://radiopaedia.org/cases/cardiomegaly) describes a massively enlarged left atrium with "compressive atelectasis of the adjacent lung bilaterally". Neither was opened and quoted, so neither counts as verified.

**4. Cardiothoracic ratio, and when heart size is unreliable**

Status: verified for the ratio and the AP and supine caveats. Not found for a statement that a pleural effusion makes heart size unreliable.

Radiopaedia, "Cardiothoracic ratio", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/cardiothoracic-ratio, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0:

> "is the ratio of maximal horizontal cardiac diameter to maximal horizontal thoracic diameter" (fragment of the definition sentence)

> "A measurement >0.50 is usually taken to be abnormal"

> "The cardiothoracic ratio should not be measured on an AP chest x-ray."

> "Objects nearer the x-ray tube appear artificially enlarged due to divergence of the x-ray beam"

> "can be due to other processes such as a pericardial effusion" (fragment, about an enlarged cardiac silhouette)

Radiology Assistant, "Chest X-Ray: Heart Failure", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/chest-x-ray/heart-failure:

> "The cardiothoracic ratio (CTR) is the ratio of the transverse diameter of the heart to the internal diameter of the chest at its widest point just above the dome of the diaphragm as measured on a PA chest film."

> "The heart size is considered too large when the CTR is > 50% on a PA chest x-ray."

> "A CTR of > 50% has a sensitivity of 50% for CHF and a specificity of 75-80%."

> "An increased cardiac silhouette is almost always the result of cardiomegaly, but occasionally it is due to pericardial effusion or even fat deposition."

> "On a supine film the cardiac silhouette will be larger due to magnification and high position of the hemidiafragms."

> "On an AP-view the VPW will increase 20% compared to a PA-view." (VPW is the vascular pedicle width, not the heart; it is the only AP-against-PA number on the page)

Radiology Assistant, "Chest X-Ray: Basic Interpretation", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/chest-x-ray/basic-interpretation:

> "When encountering an enlarged cardiac silhouette on chest radiography, it is essential to consider pericardial effusion as a potential mimic of cardiomegaly."

**5. Congestive heart failure on the chest radiograph, staged**

Status: verified, including a staged list on both sites.

Radiopaedia, "Pulmonary edema grading", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/pulmonary-oedema-grading, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0:

> "grade 0: normal chest radiograph, PCWP 8-12 mmHg"

> "grade 1: shows evidence of upper lobe diversion on a chest radiograph, PCWP 13-18 mmHg"

> "grade 2: shows interstitial edema on a chest radiograph, PCWP 19-25 mmHg"

> "grade 3: shows alveolar edema on a chest radiograph, PCWP >25 mmHg"

PCWP is pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, in mmHg.

Radiopaedia, "Congestive cardiac failure", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/congestive-cardiac-failure, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0, list items for left-sided failure: "pulmonary venous congestion", "cephalisation of pulmonary veins", "cardiomegaly (may or may not be present depending on etiology)", "pulmonary interstitial edema", "pulmonary alveolar edema", "pleural effusion".

Radiopaedia, "Pulmonary edema", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/pulmonary-oedema, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0, list items: "upper lobe pulmonary venous diversion", "septal (Kerley) lines", "peribronchial cuffing and perihilar haze", "increased cardiothoracic ratio/cardiac silhouette size", and the sentence "Pleural effusions are a frequent accompanying finding in cardiogenic/hydrostatic pulmonary edema".

Radiology Assistant, "Chest X-Ray: Heart Failure", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/chest-x-ray/heart-failure. Stage headings are "Stage I - Redistribution", "Stage II - Interstitial edema" and "Stage III - Alveolar edema", with:

> "Increased pulmonary venous pressure is related to the pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) and can be graded into stages, each with its own radiographic features on the chest film (Table)."

> "In daily clinical practice however some of these features are not seen in this sequence and sometimes may not be present at all."

> "Kerley-B lines are seen as peripheral short 1-2 cm horizontal lines near the costophrenic angles."

> "This eventually leads to fluid leakage in the alveoli (alveolar edema) and to leakage into the pleural space (pleural effusion)."

> "The following signs indicate heart failure: alveolar edema with perihilar consolidations and air bronchograms (yellow arrows); pleural fluid (blue arrow); prominent azygos vein and increased width of the vascular pedicle (red arrow) and an enlarged cardiac silhouette (arrow heads)."

> "In the supine position, there will be equalisation of blood flow, which may give the false impression of redistribution."

Radiology Assistant, "Chest X-Ray: Lung disease", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/chest-x-ray/lung-disease:

> "Look for other signs of heart failure like redistribution of pulmonary blood flow, Kerley B-lines and pleural fluid."

**6. Pleural effusion: blunted angle, meniscus, volume**

Status: verified. The two sites give different volumes, and both are quoted.

Radiopaedia, "Pleural effusion", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/pleural-effusion, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0:

> "blunting of the costophrenic angle" (list item, erect film features)

> "eventually, a meniscus will be seen, on frontal films seen laterally and gently sloping medially" (fragment; the sentence continues about hydropneumothorax)

> "it should be noted that on a routine erect chest x-ray, as much as 250-600 mL of fluid is required before it becomes evident"

> "Lateral films are able to identify a smaller amount of fluid as the costophrenic angles are deepest posteriorly."

> "Large amounts of fluid can be present on supine films with minimal imaging changes."

> "There is no meniscus, and only a veil-like increased density of the hemithorax may be visible."

Radiology Assistant, "Chest X-Ray: Heart Failure", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/chest-x-ray/heart-failure:

> "There has to be at least 175 ml of pleural fluid, before it will be visible on a PA image as a meniscus in the costophrenic angle."

> "On a lateral image effusion of > 75 ml can be visible."

> "If pleural effusion is seen on a supine chest film, it means that there is at least 500 ml present."

> "Pleural effusion is bilateral in 70% of cases of CHF."

Radiology Assistant, "Chest X-Ray: Basic Interpretation", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/chest-x-ray/basic-interpretation:

> "On chest radiography, pleural effusion typically becomes visible once 200–300 mL of fluid has accumulated."

> "Evaluate the costophrenic angles for blunting due to effusion or fibrosis."

**7. Pneumothorax, re-expansion, and drain position**

Status: verified for the pleural line, drain direction, and a drain lying outside the pleural space or in a fissure. Not found: a sentence that the lung re-expands after an intercostal drain, and any statement on side holes.

Radiopaedia, "Pneumothorax", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/pneumothorax, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0:

> "a visible visceral pleural edge, seen as a thin, soft tissue attenuation line" (fragment)

> "no lung markings peripheral to this line" (fragment)

> "Chest drain is usually indicated for any secondary spontaneous pneumothorax due to poor lung reserve"

Radiology Assistant, "Chest X-Ray: Basic Interpretation", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/chest-x-ray/basic-interpretation:

> "Chest X-ray shows a retracted visceral pleura (blue arrow), consistent with pneumothorax."

Radiopaedia, "Intercostal catheter", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/intercostal-catheter, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0:

> "insert drain with forceps directed towards the lung apex for pneumothorax or basally for hemothorax or fluid drainage" (fragment)

> "percutaneous pigtail catheters (6-8 Fr) placed under image guidance" (fragment)

The article lists "malpositioning" as a complication without saying where the tip ends up.

Radiopaedia case, "Misplaced tubes", https://radiopaedia.org/cases/misplaced-tubes, contributed by RMH Core Conditions (The Royal Melbourne Hospital), CC BY-NC-SA 3.0:

> "The left intercostal chest drain is not clearly in the pleural space."

> "While it may run in an accessory fissure, the tip lies against the mediastinum and the distal 7 cm is occluded."

> "The right intercostal chest drain terminates in the soft tissue in the right upper thorax, and is not in the pleural space."

Radiopaedia, "Re-expansion pulmonary edema", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/re-expansion-pulmonary-oedema, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0. This is the nearest thing found to re-expansion after drainage:

> "Pulmonary edema typically occurs in the setting of rapid re-expansion of a chronically collapsed lung."

> "Pre-procedure checks will identify patients at risk and rapid re-expansion should be prevented by the use of clamps or taps."

**8. Silicosis and silicotuberculosis**

Status: verified for upper-lobe nodules, progressive massive fibrosis, cavitation, and tuberculosis as a complication. The claim that silicosis raises tuberculosis risk is verified only as a Radiopaedia case sentence saying tuberculosis is reported as more common; no relative-risk number was found on either site.

Radiopaedia, "Silicosis", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/silicosis (last revised 10 Mar 2026), CC BY-NC-SA 3.0:

> "predominantly located in the upper lobe and posterior portion of the lung" (fragment)

> "large conglomerate opacities that equate to progressive massive fibrosis" (fragment)

> "Carcinoma and tuberculosis are potentially serious complications of silicosis."

> "this eggshell calcification pattern is highly suggestive of silicosis" (fragment)

Radiopaedia case, "Silicotuberculosis", https://radiopaedia.org/cases/silicotuberculosis-1, contributed by Ammar Haouimi, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0:

> "Multiple small lung nodules are noted bilaterally predominantly in the upper lobes" (fragment)

> "A cavitary lesion is noted in the apical segment of the right lower lobe" (fragment)

> "is highly suggestive of silicotuberculosis" (fragment)

> "Pulmonary tuberculosis is reported in the literature to be more common in patients with silicosis" (fragment)

Radiology Assistant, "Lung HRCT: Common diseases", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/hrct/common-diseases. This page describes HRCT, not the plain radiograph:

> "Small well-defined nodules of 2 to 5mm in diameter in both lungs."

> "Upper lobe predominance"

> "Irregular conglomerate masses, known as progressive massive fibrosis"

> "Masses may cavitate due to ischemic necrosis."

> "Infection: miliairy TB, fungus." (listed under the differential diagnosis of silicosis)

The Radiology Assistant page gives ischaemic necrosis as a cause of cavitation, and the Radiopaedia case reads a cavity in a silicotic lung as suggesting tuberculosis. Both readings are in the sources.

**9. Pages that name findings occurring together**

Status: verified for "mixed pattern", "concomitant", "combination" and "superimposed". Not found: "comorbidity", "coexisting" and "concurrent" on any page read.

Radiopaedia case, "Cryptogenic organizing pneumonia", https://radiopaedia.org/cases/39151, contributed by Henry Knipe, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0:

> "Mixed pattern of pulmonary disease with cryptogenic organizing pneumonia (COP) to be considered most likely followed by chronic eosinophilic pneumonia." (joined from pieces)

Radiopaedia, "Left lower lobe collapse", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/left-lower-lobe-collapse, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0, the sentence quoted under fact 3, which ends "or with concomitant cardiomegaly."

Radiopaedia, "Reticulonodular interstitial pattern", https://radiopaedia.org/articles/reticulonodular-pattern, CC BY-NC-SA 3.0:

> "A reticulonodular interstitial pattern is an imaging descriptive term that can be used in thoracic radiographs or CT scans" ... "when there is a combination of reticular and nodular patterns" (two separate fragments of one sentence; the words between them were not returned)

Radiology Assistant, "Chest X-Ray: Lung disease", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/chest-x-ray/lung-disease:

> "However some patients, who have an acute cardiac infarction, may still have a normal heart size, while other patients who have a large heart due to a chronic heart disease, may have non-cardiac pulmonary edema due to a superimposed pulmonay infection, ARDS, near-drowning etc."

> "Now it is obvious that some diseases can have more than one pattern."

Radiology Assistant, "Chest X-Ray: Heart Failure", https://radiologyassistant.nl/chest/chest-x-ray/heart-failure:

> "In many cases there will be both signs of RV and LV failure"
