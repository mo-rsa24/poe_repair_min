\begin{abstract}
A natural image typically depicts several distinct concepts arranged so that they form a single coherent scene, and text-to-image models generate compositional scenes from a description of their parts. Recent methods compose pretrained diffusion models at inference time by sampling from a product of experts, one expert per concept. Product-of-experts composition can fail catastrophically, producing a single blended chimera instead of a scene containing both concepts, and we trace this failure to a specific, correctable gap. This gap is the interaction term: the per-step residual between the model's joint-prompt prediction and its product-of-experts prediction, which makes explicit the signal omitted by linear score composition. We train a low-rank adapter on the cross-attention layers of Stable Diffusion XL to predict this interaction term at every denoising step without ever seeing the joint prompt and add its prediction back in as a correction. We show the correction transfers to concept pairs it was never trained on and restores scenes containing both concepts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Complex visual scenes are compositional: a single image typically depicts several distinct concepts arranged together, such as a cat beside a dog or a cup on a table, rather than any one concept in isolation. Generating such a scene from text requires not only rendering each concept accurately, but also representing the relationships among them correctly. An apple described as resting on a table, for example, should appear supported by the tabletop rather than fused into it or floating above it.

Modern text-to-image models compose multiple concepts with impressive fidelity, capturing both the appearance of individual objects and many complex relationships among them. Much of this progress has come from scaling language encoders and diffusion backbones, allowing a single model to represent an increasingly broad range of interactions (Saharia et al., 2022; Podell et al., 2023). The space of possible concept combinations nevertheless grows combinatorially and cannot be exhausted by any finite training set. An established alternative is therefore to combine pretrained diffusion models directly at inference time, without retraining on every desired combination (Liu et al., 2022).

Let $x_t$ denote the noisy latent at diffusion time $t$, and let
$s_t(x_t\mid c)=\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t\mid c)$ denote the conditional score (Song et al., 2021). Given two concept prompts $c_1$ and $c_2$, product-of-experts (PoE) composition forms a Bayes-composition ratio at every denoising step:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \hat p_t(x_t\mid c_1,c_2)
    &\propto
    \frac{p_t(x_t\mid c_1)\,p_t(x_t\mid c_2)}{p_t(x_t)}, \\
    \hat s_t(x_t\mid c_1,c_2)
    &=s_t(x_t\mid c_1)+s_t(x_t\mid c_2)-s_t(x_t).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:poe-composition}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:poe-composition} reuses the two conditional scores and subtracts their shared unconditional score once, requiring neither a joint prompt nor additional training (Liu et al., 2022). Bradley et al. (2025) identify a sufficient condition under which this linear composition is guaranteed to be projective. Their \emph{Factorized Conditionals} assumption requires each expert to modify a distinct subset of the representation, to agree with a shared base distribution outside that subset, and for the corresponding subsets to be independent under the base distribution. Under these assumptions, reverse diffusion preserves the component controlled by each expert. Natural-image concepts, however, can interact through appearance, geometry, and spatial relationships, so this factorization need not hold.

We make the resulting discrepancy explicit. Let $J$ denote the joint prompt containing both concepts, let tildes denote classifier-free-guided noise predictions, and let $w$ be the guidance scale. The score interaction $I_t$ and its equivalent correction in noise-prediction space are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    I_t(x_t;c_1,c_2)
    &:=s_t(x_t\mid J)-\hat s_t(x_t\mid c_1,c_2), \\
    r_t
    &:=\widetilde{\epsilon}_{J}(x_t,t)
      -\widetilde{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{PoE}}(x_t,t)
      =-w\sigma_t I_t(x_t;c_1,c_2), \\
    \widetilde{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{PoE}}
    &:=\widetilde{\epsilon}_{1}
      +\widetilde{\epsilon}_{2}
      -\widetilde{\epsilon}_{\emptyset}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:interaction-term}
\end{equation}
Thus, $r_t$ is precisely the per-step residual between the model's joint-prompt prediction and its PoE prediction, with the known score-to-noise and guidance scaling $w\sigma_t$. Our central question is whether this missing interaction can be predicted without evaluating the joint prompt.

Figure~\ref{fig:poe-failure} makes the failure concrete. The schematic shows how multiplying two overlapping concept experts can favor an intermediate configuration, while the corresponding SDXL sample combines visual attributes of a cat and a dog in a single animal. This example does not by itself establish that Factorized Conditionals are violated; rather, it exhibits the kind of interference that their independence structure is designed to exclude and motivates measuring the omitted interaction directly.

To test whether $r_t$ contains the relevant missing signal, we add it back into the PoE sampler:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\epsilon}^{(\lambda)}_t
    =\widetilde{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{PoE}}+\lambda r_t,
    \qquad \lambda\in[0,1].
\label{eq:correction-strength}
\end{equation}
Here $\lambda=0$ recovers plain PoE, whereas $\lambda=1$ recovers the joint-prompt prediction at every visited state. We compare the matched residual with three controls of comparable size: a residual from another concept pair, one from another seed, and the matched residual with its denoising steps shuffled.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.94\linewidth]{F2-correction-strength.png}
\caption{\textbf{The matched interaction correction restores composition; the controls do not.} \textbf{Top:} cat $\times$ dog, seed 9, as increasing amounts of the correction are added. The matched correction turns the blended animal into a two-animal scene. \textbf{Bottom:} the same pattern holds across the evaluation set: the matched correction moves toward two-object scenes, while the wrong-pair, wrong-seed, and shuffled-step controls stay close to the original PoE failure mode.}
\label{fig:correction-strength}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:correction-strength} shows that the effect grows as more of the matched correction is added. The matched residual changes the output from a blended animal into two distinct animals. The controls do not have the same effect, even though they are made comparable in size. These interventions show that composition is not recovered by just any similarly sized change: in this setting, it depends on the content, trajectory, and temporal order carried by $r_t$. The remainder of this paper makes that correction available without querying the joint prompt.

\begin{samepage}
\textbf{Contributions.} This paper makes three contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{We define the missing interaction.} We write $r_t$ as the step-by-step difference between the model's joint-prompt prediction and its PoE prediction, turning the missing compositional signal into a quantity we can measure and correct.
    \item \textbf{We show the residual is the signal that restores composition.} Adding the correct residual turns PoE failures into two-object scenes; mismatched residuals of comparable size do not have this effect.
    \item \textbf{We learn a lightweight correction that transfers.} A low-rank adapter on SDXL's cross-attention layers predicts $r_t$ without access to the joint prompt and composes concept pairs excluded from its training set.
\end{itemize}
\end{samepage}
